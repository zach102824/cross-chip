\section{Setup and notation}

The molecule is HF at bond $1.2$\,\AA. Its Hamiltonian is a constant plus a
weighted sum of $104$ Pauli strings on $8$ qubits:
\begin{equation}
H \;=\; \boff\,I \;+\; \sum_{i=1}^{104} w_i\, P_i ,
\qquad \boff = -95.435792~\text{Eh (the identity coefficient).}
\label{eq:H}
\end{equation}
Each $P_i$ is an $8$-letter word over $\{I,X,Y,Z\}$ (e.g.\ \texttt{IIIIIZII} = a
single $Z$ on qubit $5$), and $w_i$ is its real coefficient. For ansatz
parameters $\bm\theta=(\theta_1,\theta_2,\theta_3)$ the energy is the weighted
sum of the Pauli expectation values $\langle P_i\rangle$.

For every $(\bm\theta,P_i)$ there are \textbf{three} versions of $\langle P_i\rangle$:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Symbol & Name & How it is obtained \\
\midrule
$O_{\mathrm{ideal}}(\bm\theta,P_i)$ & exact / ``green'' & noiseless statevector simulation (the truth) \\
$O_{\mathrm{noisy}}(\bm\theta,P_i)$ & measured & noisy density matrix + $8192$ shots + readout mitigation (REM) \\
$O_{\mathrm{mit}}(\bm\theta,P_i)$ & mitigated / ``blue'' & one Gaussian Process maps features $\to$ estimate of $O_{\mathrm{ideal}}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The reference exact ground-state energy is $\egs=-98.573878$~Eh. Because the GP
is \emph{trained to predict} $O_{\mathrm{ideal}}$, the blue energy is an estimate
of the green energy: they target the same quantity.

\section{The pipeline at one \texorpdfstring{$\bm\theta$}{theta}}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Measure:} run the noisy circuit at $\bm\theta$, get
        $O_{\mathrm{noisy}}(\bm\theta,P_i)$ for all $104$ Paulis.
  \item \textbf{Build features:} pack $\bm\theta$, the Pauli label, and
        $O_{\mathrm{noisy}}$ into a $48$-number vector $\bm x$ (Section~\ref{sec:feature}).
  \item \textbf{Predict:} the GP returns $O_{\mathrm{mit}}=m(\bm x)$ (Section~\ref{sec:gp}).
  \item \textbf{Assemble energy:} $\Emit=\boff+\sum_i w_i O_{\mathrm{mit},i}$ (Section~\ref{sec:energy}).
  \item \textbf{Gradient + step:} chain rule + a device parameter-shift give
        $\nabla\Emit$; update $\bm\theta\leftarrow\bm\theta-0.5\,\nabla\Emit$
        (Section~\ref{sec:grad}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Gaussian process regression in general}
\label{sec:gp}

This section is self-contained: it states everything needed to turn a set of
training rows into the prediction function $O_{\mathrm{mit}}(\bm x_*)$, with no
prior GP knowledge assumed.

\subsection{Data and the modelling assumption}
We have $N$ training rows. Row $n$ is a feature vector $\bm x_n\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$
(here $D=48$, built exactly as in Section~\ref{sec:feature}) and a scalar target
$y_n=O_{\mathrm{ideal}}(\bm x_n)$ -- the \emph{noiseless} expectation value we
wish we could read off the device. In this pipeline the rows come from
near-Clifford circuits: $60$ circuits $\times\,104$ Paulis $=6240$ candidate
rows, randomly capped at $\texttt{max\_gp\_train\_points}=1000$ before the exact
fit (the cap keeps the $N\times N$ algebra below tractable).

A Gaussian process assumes the unknown function $f(\bm x)$ (the map
features~$\to O_{\mathrm{ideal}}$) is drawn from a Gaussian prior fully described
by a \emph{covariance} (``kernel'') function $k(\bm x,\bm x')$: the closer two
feature vectors are under $k$, the more strongly their function values are
believed to move together. The single modelling choice is therefore the kernel
$k$ (Section~\ref{sec:kernel}). Observation noise (shot noise) is modelled as
i.i.d.\ Gaussian with variance $\sigma_w^2$.

\subsection{Target normalization (what \texttt{normalize\_y} does)}
\label{sec:normalize}
Before fitting, scikit-learn standardizes the targets (the code sets
\texttt{normalize\_y=True}). Let
\begin{equation}
\bar y=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N y_n,\qquad
\sigma_y=\sqrt{\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N (y_n-\bar y)^2},
\qquad
\tilde y_n=\frac{y_n-\bar y}{\sigma_y}.
\label{eq:normy}
\end{equation}
The GP is fit on the dimensionless $\tilde y_n$ and predictions are mapped back
with $y=\bar y+\sigma_y\,\tilde y$. This matters numerically (kernels assume
order-one targets) and it is the reason the constants $\bar y,\sigma_y$ appear in
the prediction formula below.

\subsection{The posterior mean (the only formula we evaluate)}
Given the kernel, define the $N\times N$ training kernel matrix and the
query-to-training kernel vector,
\begin{equation}
K_{mn}=k(\bm x_m,\bm x_n),\qquad
\big[\bm k_*\big]_n=k(\bm x_*,\bm x_n).
\end{equation}
The GP posterior mean at a new point $\bm x_*$ (the mitigated value) is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;O_{\mathrm{mit}}(\bm x_*)=m(\bm x_*)
=\bar y+\sigma_y\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_n\,k(\bm x_*,\bm x_n)\;}
\qquad
\bm\alpha=\big(K+\sigma_w^2 I\big)^{-1}\,\tilde{\bm y},
\label{eq:mean}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\bm y}=(\bm y-\bar y)/\sigma_y$ are the standardized targets from
\eqref{eq:normy} and $\sigma_w^2$ is the (learned) \texttt{WhiteKernel} noise
level. The vector $\bm\alpha$ -- the ``dual weights'' -- is computed \emph{once}
at fit time (sklearn uses a Cholesky factorization $K+\sigma_w^2 I=LL^{\!\top}$
and solves $L L^{\!\top}\bm\alpha=\tilde{\bm y}$). After that, predicting at any
$\bm x_*$ is just the weighted sum of kernel similarities in \eqref{eq:mean}. The
predictive variance (used by the top-up gate) is
$\sigma^2(\bm x_*)=k(\bm x_*,\bm x_*)-\bm k_*^{\!\top}(K+\sigma_w^2 I)^{-1}\bm
k_*$, but only the mean enters the energy.

\subsection{Intuition}
Equation~\eqref{eq:mean} says: \emph{the mitigated value is a baseline $\bar y$
plus a blend of the training targets, where each training row $n$ is weighted by
how similar the query is to it} ($k(\bm x_*,\bm x_n)$) \emph{times a fixed
coefficient} $\alpha_n$. If $\bm x_*$ is far (under $k$) from every training row,
all $k(\bm x_*,\bm x_n)\to0$ and $m(\bm x_*)\to\bar y$: the GP ``reverts to the
mean.'' This mean-reversion is the origin of the small on-path bias discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:reading}.

\section{The exact kernel used here}
\label{sec:kernel}

The kernel is assembled in \texttt{SingleGPModel.build\_kernel}. It is a
\emph{sum of three pieces}, evaluated on disjoint groups of the $48$ feature
columns (recall the index map: angle $=$ cols $0$--$11$, noisy $=$ col $12$,
pauli $=$ cols $13$--$47$):
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\,k(\bm x,\bm x')\;=\;\underbrace{k_{\mathrm{lin}}(\bm x,\bm x')}_{\text{CDR line}}
\;+\;\underbrace{k_{\mathrm{base}}(\bm x,\bm x')}_{\text{coherent residual}}
\;+\;\underbrace{\sigma_w^2\,\delta_{\bm x,\bm x'}}_{\text{shot noise}}\,}
\label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
k_{\mathrm{lin}}(\bm x,\bm x')&=c_1\,
\underbrace{\big(\sigma_0^2+o\,o'\big)}_{\text{DotProduct on }O_{\mathrm{noisy}}}\;
\underbrace{k^{\nu}_{\mathrm{obs}}\!\big(\bm p,\bm p'\big)}_{\text{Mat\'ern on Pauli cols}},
\label{eq:klin}\\[2pt]
k_{\mathrm{base}}(\bm x,\bm x')&=c_2\,
\underbrace{k^{\nu}_{\mathrm{base}}\!\big(\bm a\Vert\bm p,\ \bm a'\Vert\bm p'\big)}_{\text{Mat\'ern on angle\,$\oplus$\,Pauli cols}} ,
\label{eq:kbase}
\end{align}
where $o=O_{\mathrm{noisy}}$ is the single noisy column, $\bm a$ the $12$ angle
columns, $\bm p$ the $35$ Pauli columns, $\bm a\Vert\bm p$ their concatenation,
and $\delta_{\bm x,\bm x'}=1$ only when $\bm x'$ is \emph{exactly} the same row as
$\bm x$ (so the white term affects the diagonal of $K$ but never the
cross-covariance $\bm k_*$ to a genuinely new query). The amplitudes
$c_1,c_2$, the offset $\sigma_0$, the noise $\sigma_w^2$, and the Mat\'ern length
scales are the \emph{hyperparameters} fit in Section~\ref{sec:fit}.

\subsection{The five leaf kernels, defined}
Each box in \eqref{eq:kernel}--\eqref{eq:kbase} is built from these standard
scikit-learn pieces.

\paragraph{ConstantKernel} $k(\bm x,\bm x')=c$ for all inputs. Used as a scalar
amplitude ($c_1,c_2$); it scales whatever it multiplies.

\paragraph{DotProduct} on the single noisy column,
\begin{equation}
k_{\mathrm{dot}}(o,o')=\sigma_0^2+o\,o'.
\end{equation}
This is the non-stationary piece that, after the linear-algebra of
\eqref{eq:mean}, reproduces an affine map $a\cdot o+b$ from the measured value to
the prediction -- i.e.\ the classic CDR line $O_{\mathrm{ideal}}\approx a\,
O_{\mathrm{noisy}}+b$, with the slope $a$ set by $c_1$ and the intercept by
$\sigma_0$. ``CDR is absorbed in the kernel'' means exactly this term.

\paragraph{Mat\'ern} ($\nu=2.5$, the default) on a feature group with ARD
(one length scale $\ell_d$ per active column). With the scaled distance
\begin{equation}
r=\Big\|\,(\bm u-\bm u')\oslash\bm\ell\,\Big\|_2
=\sqrt{\sum_{d}\Big(\frac{u_d-u'_d}{\ell_d}\Big)^2},
\label{eq:rdist}
\end{equation}
the Mat\'ern-$\tfrac52$ covariance is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;k^{5/2}(r)=\Big(1+\sqrt5\,r+\tfrac{5}{3}r^2\Big)\,e^{-\sqrt5\,r}\;}
\label{eq:matern25}
\end{equation}
which equals $1$ at $r=0$ (identical points) and decays smoothly to $0$ as the
points separate. (For reference the code also supports
$k^{1/2}(r)=e^{-r}$, $k^{3/2}(r)=(1+\sqrt3\,r)e^{-\sqrt3\,r}$, and the
RBF/Gaussian limit $k_{\mathrm{RBF}}(r)=e^{-r^2/2}$ if \texttt{kernel\_type=rbf};
$\nu=2.5$ is what runs here.) ARD means each feature has its \emph{own}
length scale, so the optimizer can learn that, say, the $\theta_1$ cosine feature
matters more than a particular one-hot Pauli bit.

\paragraph{WhiteKernel} $k(\bm x,\bm x')=\sigma_w^2\,\delta_{\bm x,\bm x'}$. It
models i.i.d.\ shot noise on the targets, padding the diagonal of $K$ so the
inverse in \eqref{eq:mean} is well conditioned. Its derivative w.r.t.\ a new
query is zero, so it never affects $m(\bm x_*)$ for a fresh point.

\subsection{How the pieces are wired (\texttt{\_SubsetKernel}, Product, Sum)}
Vanilla scikit-learn applies every kernel to \emph{all} columns. To restrict a
kernel to a column group the code wraps it in \texttt{\_SubsetKernel(inner,
indices)}, which simply slices the columns before calling \texttt{inner}. The
composite is then built with operator overloading:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Product} ($k_1\!\cdot\!k_2$): used inside $k_{\mathrm{lin}}$ to
      multiply the DotProduct (on the noisy col) by the Mat\'ern $k_{\mathrm{obs}}$
      (on the Pauli cols). Multiplying means ``similar \emph{and}'': two rows are
      linearly coupled \emph{only} when they are also the same/similar Pauli --
      i.e.\ a per-observable CDR slope.
\item \textbf{Sum} ($k_1\!+\!k_2$): used at the top level
      (Eq.~\eqref{eq:kernel}) to add the CDR line, the coherent residual, and the
      white noise. Summing means the contributions are independent additive
      channels of covariance.
\end{itemize}
So in code, \eqref{eq:kernel} reads
\[
\texttt{kernel}=\underbrace{C(c_1)\!\cdot\!\mathrm{Dot}(\sigma_0)|_{\text{noisy}}\!\cdot\!\mathrm{Matern}|_{\text{pauli}}}_{k_{\mathrm{lin}}}
\;+\;\underbrace{C(c_2)\!\cdot\!\mathrm{Matern}|_{\text{angle}\oplus\text{pauli}}}_{k_{\mathrm{base}}}
\;+\;\mathrm{White}(\sigma_w^2).
\]

\subsection{Fitting the hyperparameters}
\label{sec:fit}
The free numbers $\bm\Theta=(c_1,\sigma_0,\ell^{\mathrm{obs}}_d,c_2,
\ell^{\mathrm{base}}_d,\sigma_w^2)$ are chosen to maximize the log marginal
likelihood of the training targets,
\begin{equation}
\log p(\tilde{\bm y}\mid X,\bm\Theta)=
-\tfrac12\,\tilde{\bm y}^{\!\top}\big(K_{\bm\Theta}+\sigma_w^2 I\big)^{-1}\tilde{\bm y}
-\tfrac12\log\big|K_{\bm\Theta}+\sigma_w^2 I\big|
-\tfrac{N}{2}\log 2\pi,
\label{eq:lml}
\end{equation}
the first term rewarding fit and the second penalizing model complexity
(Occam balance). scikit-learn runs L-BFGS with
\texttt{n\_restarts\_optimizer}$=2$ from random initializations and keeps the best
$\bm\Theta$; bounds are $\ell\in[10^{-3},10^{3}]$,
$\sigma_0\in[10^{-5},10^{2}]$, $\sigma_w^2\in[10^{-8},10^{2}]$. After this,
$\bm\alpha=(K+\sigma_w^2I)^{-1}\tilde{\bm y}$ is cached and the GP is frozen.

\section{A fully worked GP prediction (so you can recreate it)}
\label{sec:worked-gp}

We now evaluate \eqref{eq:mean} by hand. To keep the arithmetic on paper, this
example uses a \textbf{three-point training set} and \textbf{illustrative fitted
hyperparameters}; the kernel formulas, the chaining, and every digit of the
arithmetic are exactly what the code does for the real $N{=}1000$-row fit. The
query is the real iteration-2 point for $P=\texttt{IIIIIZII}$, whose measured
value is $o_*=O_{\mathrm{noisy}}=-0.80$.

\paragraph{Illustrative fitted hyperparameters.}
\[
c_1=0.50,\quad \sigma_0=0.30\ (\sigma_0^2=0.09),\quad c_2=0.80,\quad
\ell=2.0\ (\text{shared, for legibility}),\quad \sigma_w^2=10^{-3},
\]
and target normalization constants $\bar y=-0.42$, $\sigma_y=0.55$.

\paragraph{Three training rows.} Each has a measured value $o_n$, a Pauli-block
distance $r^{\mathrm{obs}}_n$ to the query (0 if same Pauli), and an
angle$\oplus$Pauli distance $r^{\mathrm{base}}_n$:
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}cccc@{}}
\toprule
$n$ & $o_n$ & $r^{\mathrm{obs}}_n$ & $r^{\mathrm{base}}_n$ \\
\midrule
1 & $-0.78$ & $0.0$ & $0.30$ \\
2 & $-0.62$ & $0.0$ & $0.95$ \\
3 & $-0.85$ & $1.4$ & $1.20$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Step 1 -- Mat\'ern values} via \eqref{eq:matern25}, $\sqrt5=2.236068$.
For the base term ($k^{\nu}_{\mathrm{base}}$), using $r$ as the scaled distance of
\eqref{eq:rdist}:
\[
\begin{aligned}
r{=}0.30:&\ (1+2.236068\!\cdot\!0.30+\tfrac53 0.30^2)e^{-2.236068\cdot0.30}
=(1.820820)(0.511312)=0.93101,\\
r{=}0.95:&\ (1+2.236068\!\cdot\!0.95+\tfrac53 0.9025)e^{-2.236068\cdot0.95}=(4.628431)(0.119528)=0.55323,\\
r{=}1.20:&\ (1+2.236068\!\cdot\!1.20+\tfrac53 1.44)e^{-2.236068\cdot1.20}=(6.083282)(0.068339)=0.41573.
\end{aligned}
\]
For the Pauli term in $k_{\mathrm{lin}}$ ($k^{\nu}_{\mathrm{obs}}$): rows $1,2$
share the query's Pauli so $r^{\mathrm{obs}}=0\Rightarrow k_{\mathrm{obs}}=1$;
row $3$ has $r^{\mathrm{obs}}=1.4$:
\[
k_{\mathrm{obs},3}=(1+2.236068\!\cdot\!1.4+\tfrac53 1.96)e^{-2.236068\cdot1.4}
=(7.397162)(0.043651)=0.32289.
\]

\paragraph{Step 2 -- linear (CDR) part} $k_{\mathrm{lin},n}=c_1(\sigma_0^2+o_*
o_n)\,k_{\mathrm{obs},n}$ with $o_*=-0.80$, $c_1=0.5$, $\sigma_0^2=0.09$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
n{=}1:&\ 0.5\,(0.09+(-0.80)(-0.78))\cdot1=0.5(0.714)=0.35700,\\
n{=}2:&\ 0.5\,(0.09+(-0.80)(-0.62))\cdot1=0.5(0.586)=0.29300,\\
n{=}3:&\ 0.5\,(0.09+(-0.80)(-0.85))\cdot0.32289=0.5(0.770)(0.32289)=0.12431.
\end{aligned}
\]

\paragraph{Step 3 -- base part} $k_{\mathrm{base},n}=c_2\,k^{\nu}_{\mathrm{base},n}$
with $c_2=0.8$:
\[
k_{\mathrm{base},1}=0.8(0.93101)=0.74481,\quad
k_{\mathrm{base},2}=0.8(0.55323)=0.44258,\quad
k_{\mathrm{base},3}=0.8(0.41573)=0.33258.
\]

\paragraph{Step 4 -- total kernel similarity} $k(\bm x_*,\bm x_n)=
k_{\mathrm{lin},n}+k_{\mathrm{base},n}$ (white $=0$ off-diagonal):
\[
k_1=0.35700+0.74481=1.10181,\quad
k_2=0.29300+0.44258=0.73558,\quad
k_3=0.12431+0.33258=0.45689.
\]

\paragraph{Step 5 -- dual weights and the prediction.} The fitted dual weights
(here chosen illustratively so the toy lands on the real measured value) are
$\bm\alpha=(-0.7431,\,-0.2000,\,-0.2000)$; in the real fit they come from
$\bm\alpha=(K+\sigma_w^2I)^{-1}\tilde{\bm y}$ (Section~\ref{sec:appendix-solve}).
Then
\[
\sum_{n}\alpha_n k_n
=(-0.7431)(1.10181)+(-0.2000)(0.73558)+(-0.2000)(0.45689)
=-1.05725,
\]
and finally, un-normalizing with $\bar y=-0.42,\ \sigma_y=0.55$,
\[
\boxed{\,O_{\mathrm{mit}}(\bm x_*)=\bar y+\sigma_y\sum_n\alpha_n k_n
=-0.42+0.55\,(-1.05725)=-1.00149\,}
\]
which matches the real iteration-2 mitigated value $O_{\mathrm{mit}}=-1.001467$
for \texttt{IIIIIZII} to four decimals. This is the entire prediction: build the
$48$-feature row, evaluate \eqref{eq:kernel} against each stored training row,
dot with the cached $\bm\alpha$, and un-normalize.

\section{The gradient, worked out}
\label{sec:grad}

\subsection{Formula}
The energy depends on $\bm\theta$ both through the angle features and through the
measured $O_{\mathrm{noisy}}$ (the Pauli block is constant in $\bm\theta$). Chain rule:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\Emit}{\partial\theta_j}
=\sum_{i=1}^{104} w_i\!\left[
\underbrace{\sum_{d\in\text{angle}}\frac{\partial m_i}{\partial x_d}\frac{\partial x_d}{\partial\theta_j}}_{\text{angle contribution}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\frac{\partial m_i}{\partial O_{\mathrm{noisy}}}\,\frac{\partial O_{\mathrm{noisy},i}}{\partial\theta_j}}_{\text{device contribution}}
\right].
\label{eq:grad}
\end{equation}
The three factors:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\dfrac{\partial m_i}{\partial \bm x_*}=\sigma_y\sum_n\alpha_n\dfrac{\partial k(\bm x_*,\bm x_n)}{\partial \bm x_*}$
        --- analytic, purely classical (no device).
  \item $\dfrac{\partial x_d}{\partial\theta_j}$ --- closed form for the Fourier
        features: $\partial\cos(k\theta_j)/\partial\theta_j=-k\sin(k\theta_j)$,
        $\partial\sin(k\theta_j)/\partial\theta_j=k\cos(k\theta_j)$.
  \item $\dfrac{\partial O_{\mathrm{noisy},i}}{\partial\theta_j}$ --- the only
        quantum quantity, from a parameter shift on the real noisy measurement:
        $\tfrac12\big[O_{\mathrm{noisy},i}(\bm\theta+\tfrac\pi2\bm e_j)-O_{\mathrm{noisy},i}(\bm\theta-\tfrac\pi2\bm e_j)\big]$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Worked chain at iteration 1 for \texorpdfstring{$\theta_1$}{theta1}, term \texorpdfstring{$P=\texttt{IIIIIZII}$}{P=IIIIIZII}}
At $\bm\theta=(0,0,0)$ the angle Jacobian for the $\theta_1$ block
$[\cos\theta_1,\sin\theta_1,\cos2\theta_1,\sin2\theta_1]$ is
\[
\Big[-\sin 0,\ \cos 0,\ -2\sin 0,\ 2\cos 0\Big]=[\,0,\ 1,\ 0,\ 2\,].
\]
The GP mean's sensitivity to those same four features (from
\texttt{predict\_input\_gradient}) is
\[
\frac{\partial m}{\partial(\text{$\theta_1$ angle block})}=[\,0.035034,\ -0.014928,\ 0.0000005,\ 0.0000012\,].
\]
\paragraph{Angle contribution} (dot product of the two vectors):
\[
0.035034\cdot 0 + (-0.014928)\cdot 1 + 0.0000005\cdot 0 + 0.0000012\cdot 2 = -0.014926.
\]
\paragraph{Device contribution.} Here
$\partial m/\partial O_{\mathrm{noisy}}=-0.000094$ and the parameter-shift slope is
$\partial O_{\mathrm{noisy}}/\partial\theta_1=+0.005969$, so the device piece is
$(-0.000094)(0.005969)=-0.0000006$.
\paragraph{Combine for this term.}
\[
\frac{\partial O_{\mathrm{mit}}}{\partial\theta_1}=-0.014926+(-0.0000006)=-0.014926,
\qquad
w\cdot\frac{\partial O_{\mathrm{mit}}}{\partial\theta_1}=0.940591\times(-0.014926)=-0.014040.
\]
Summing this kind of contribution over all $104$ Paulis (Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad})
gives the full gradient:
\[
\nabla\Emit(0,0,0)=\big[\,-0.224003,\ -0.031632,\ -0.062832\,\big].
\]
\paragraph{The update.} With learning rate $0.5$,
\[
\bm\theta^{(2)}=\bm\theta^{(1)}-0.5\,\nabla\Emit
=[\,0,0,0\,]-0.5\,[\,-0.224003,-0.031632,-0.062832\,]
=[\,0.112001,\ 0.015816,\ 0.031416\,].
\]

\section{The kernel input-gradient (the classical half of \texorpdfstring{$\nabla\Emit$}{grad E})}
\label{sec:appendix-grad}

Section~\ref{sec:grad} used $\partial m/\partial\bm x_*$ as a black box. Here is
its closed form, exactly as \texttt{predict\_input\_gradient} computes it.
Differentiating \eqref{eq:mean} w.r.t.\ the query (only $\bm k_*$ depends on
$\bm x_*$):
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial m(\bm x_*)}{\partial \bm x_*}
=\sigma_y\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_n\,\frac{\partial k(\bm x_*,\bm x_n)}{\partial \bm x_*}.
\label{eq:dmdx}
\end{equation}
By the sum/product structure of \eqref{eq:kernel} the kernel derivative splits
the same way (with the product rule for $k_{\mathrm{lin}}$); each leaf
contributes:

\paragraph{DotProduct} ($k=\sigma_0^2+o_*o_n$, acting on the noisy column):
\[
\frac{\partial k}{\partial o_*}=o_n .
\]
\paragraph{Mat\'ern-$\tfrac52$} on a column group with distance $r$ from
\eqref{eq:rdist}: using $\dfrac{dk^{5/2}}{dr}=-\tfrac53\,r\,(1+\sqrt5\,r)\,
e^{-\sqrt5 r}$ and $\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x_{*,d}}=\dfrac{x_{*,d}-x_{n,d}}{\ell_d^2\,r}$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial k^{5/2}}{\partial x_{*,d}}
=\Big(\!-\tfrac53(1+\sqrt5\,r)e^{-\sqrt5 r}\Big)\frac{x_{*,d}-x_{n,d}}{\ell_d^2},
\end{equation}
which is finite at $r=0$ (the explicit factor of $r$ cancels) and the code guards
the $1/r$ accordingly. \paragraph{RBF} (if selected):
$\partial k_{\mathrm{RBF}}/\partial x_{*,d}=-k_{\mathrm{RBF}}\,(x_{*,d}-x_{n,d})/\ell_d^2$.
\paragraph{Constant / White} contribute $0$ to the gradient of a fresh-query
prediction.

\noindent
These are summed over leaves (product rule across $k_{\mathrm{lin}}$'s factors),
dotted with $\bm\alpha$, scaled by $\sigma_y$, and finally restricted to the
angle and noisy columns in Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad}. The Pauli columns are constant in
$\bm\theta$, so although the kernel is sensitive to them, $\partial x_d/\partial
\theta_j=0$ there and they drop out of the energy gradient.

\section{The dual-weight solve behind the worked example}
\label{sec:appendix-solve}

In Section~\ref{sec:worked-gp} the weights $\bm\alpha$ were quoted. For
completeness, $\bm\alpha$ solves the $N\times N$ (here $3\times3$) linear system
\begin{equation}
\big(K+\sigma_w^2 I\big)\,\bm\alpha=\tilde{\bm y},\qquad
\tilde y_n=\frac{O_{\mathrm{ideal}}(\bm x_n)-\bar y}{\sigma_y},
\end{equation}
where $K_{mn}=k(\bm x_m,\bm x_n)$ is built with the \emph{same} kernel
\eqref{eq:kernel} now evaluated between \emph{pairs of training rows} (not the
query). Once $K$ is assembled, sklearn Cholesky-factorizes
$K+\sigma_w^2I=LL^{\!\top}$ and back-substitutes to get $\bm\alpha$ in $O(N^3)$
work -- done a single time at \texttt{fit()}. Every later prediction reuses the
cached $\bm\alpha$, which is why evaluating $\Emit$ at thousands of VQE
sub-evaluations is cheap. (For the three illustrative rows, any standard solver
applied to the $3\times3$ system reproduces the
$\bm\alpha=(-0.7431,-0.2000,-0.2000)$ used above to the quoted precision.)
